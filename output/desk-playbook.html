What the whole genre still doesn't do: show a full graded record. Wins get pinned, losses get quiet. Directory listings put dozens of these communities at $10–100/month, with sportsbook affiliate CPA (often $200+ per signup) frequently out-earning subscriptions in year one. The receipts gap is the opening.

02 The improvement: receipts as the brand

You have something the genre can't fake: an automated desk that logs every pick with the price taken and the closing price, grades it without human hands, and publishes the record to a page nobody edits. The brand thesis writes itself — every pick graded in public, wins and losses, tracked against the closing line. It converts the exact skepticism that makes people hate cappers into the reason to follow this one. Losing recaps get posted with the same production quality as winning ones; the CLV receipt post each Monday is a content format no competitor runs.

Home runs still power growth — longshot props and small-unit parlays are legitimate marketing inventory when they're labeled as lottery plays, logged like everything else, and sized honestly. When one hits, that graphic does the advertising.

Hard lines (also the commercially correct ones): no fabricated or trimmed records, no purchased followers or bot engagement, no "guaranteed winner" claims, no deleting losses. Beyond ethics, X throttles inauthentic engagement, communities detect it fast, and one screenshot of a deleted loss ends a picks brand. On Grok-style reply bots: automated posting of your own content is fine and X requires automated accounts to be labeled; automated fake engagement risks the account. Skip it — the content engine below gives you volume without it.

03 Funnel and money

X → free Discord → paid tier. Free side always gets real picks (the record must be public to mean anything); paid sells speed and completeness — flags the moment they fire, the full board, line-move alerts, and the daily slip content the genre actually pays for. The field notes force one product change: sharp spreads alone don't sell at $10/week — the paid tier needs a daily slips lab (SGPs and prop ladders built from the model's leans, clearly labeled by tier: sharp flags vs lottery plays, every one logged). The desk gives the credibility; the slips give the dopamine; the record covers both honestly.

Pricing follows the genre: $9.99/week via Whop with a $29.99/month option and a season pass, launching around Week 4 once four weeks of graded receipts exist. Weekly billing is the genre standard for a reason — match it. Add sportsbook affiliate links only after the audience exists, with clear disclosure.

Persona call — yours to make

Wavey's bio sells a person, and it works. Options: (a) you as the face — strongest trust, needs your voice in replies and the occasional video; (b) a branded desk voice — "the desk says" — fully automatable and honest about what it is, colder but on-thesis for a receipts brand. What I won't run is an invented human pretending to be real — fabricated-person marketing breaks the same trust the record is built on (and X labels automated accounts anyway). A mascot or desk identity that's openly a brand is fine and can carry swagger.

One brand. NFL is the flagship — launch now, season starts in eight days. Add NBA under the same handle in late October: it solves the dead-days problem (daily inventory Monday–Saturday) and multiplies paid value without splitting the audience. 04b Persona & authenticity

The thing that makes this work and the thing you're worried about are the same thing: there is a real person here doing real work. You built an EPA model, ran a walk-forward backtest, and shop lines. That's more genuine effort than 95% of this industry puts in. The persona isn't a costume over automation — it's the true story, surfaced well. That distinction is also what keeps it off the wrong side of X's rules.

What actually gets flagged (and what doesn't)

X's platform-manipulation policy targets fake engagement and inauthentic behavior — bought followers, engagement pods, mass identical replies, coordinated botting, hundreds of automated actions an hour. It does not target a real person scheduling their own original content through a tool. Half of professional Twitter posts through Typefully, Hypefury, or the API. The "automated account" label X asks for is for fully-hands-off bot accounts (think an earthquake-alert bot), not for you writing and approving a card that posts at 10am. You are a person using a scheduler — that's the normal case, not the edge case.

Stay clean by behaving like the real account you are: a human (you) reviews and approves before anything posts; replies and DMs are genuinely yours; posts vary in wording and timing rather than firing identical templates on a metronome; growth comes from content and real collabs, never bought followers or pods. Do that and there's nothing to "get away with" — you're not simulating a person, you are one.

The one line we don't cross

Inventing a fictional human — a fake name, a fake backstory, a stock-photo face — and passing it off as real is the one move that detonates a receipts brand. If it's ever uncovered (and betting communities are relentless about this), the fabrication contaminates the record too: "if the girl-dad thing was fake, the record's probably fake." You've said you don't want that, and it's also the commercially correct call. Everything below is built from things that are true about you.

Recommended persona: the sharp who shows his work

The betting-content field splits into four archetypes: the everyman grinder (relatable, fast growth, high churn), the degenerate entertainer (chaos and big parlays), the mentor/teacher (educational), and the quant/sharp (credibility-first, slower but stickier and higher-trust). Wavey runs everyman-entertainer. Your unfair advantage is the fourth lane, which almost nobody in the picks space occupies well because almost nobody has a real model — the sharp who shows his work. Then warm it with personality so it isn't a cold spreadsheet.

Why this one wins for you specifically: it's the only archetype where "hard work going into this" is literally demonstrable content — you can post the model, the process, the line-shop, the CLV math. Transparency stops being a slogan and becomes the format. It's defensible (competitors can't copy a model they don't have). And it ages well: entertainers live and die on the current streak, a sharp's trust compounds across a whole season.
